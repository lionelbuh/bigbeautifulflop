Switzerland & France

Valaisan (Switzerland)Valais: Sion, Martigny, Sierre

Satire

Adieu ! J’étais pas sur le bulletin. Si j’y avais été, on gagnait par avalanche. Pas par glissement de terrain : par avalanche.

Ceux d’en bas peuvent bien voter ce qu’ils veulent. Chez nous, on monte voter à pied, on redescend pour la raclette, et le résultat, on le connaît avant même d’ouvrir l’urne. Merci de votre attention, et fermez la porte, vous laissez entrer le froid.

English version

Adieu! I wasn’t on the ballot. If I had been, we’d have won by an avalanche. Not a landslide: an avalanche.

The people down in the lowlands can vote however they like. Up here we walk up to vote, walk back down for the raclette, and we know the result before the ballot box is even opened. Thank you for your attention, and shut the door, you’re letting the cold in.

I wasn’t on the ballot. And if I had been, we’d have won, and not by a little: a landslide.

Well, I’m just saying, I’m not saying anything. Round here we say “there’s nobody like us”, but we say it quietly, between two glasses of Chasselas, not in capital letters. So let’s say we would have won… we’ll see. Thank you for your attention, whenever you have a moment.

Satire

J’étais même pas sur le bulletin. Et si j’y avais été, on gagnait par K.-O., direct, au premier tour.

Mais bon, voter, faut y aller : la ligne 13 est blindée, le bureau de vote est au fond d’une école, et l’assesseur te regarde comme si tu lui devais de l’argent. Moi, j’aurais gagné sans même me déplacer. Merci de votre attention, enfin, si vous avez cinq minutes, parce que moi je suis pressé.

English version

I wasn’t even on the ballot. And if I had been, we’d have won by knockout, straight away, in the first round.

But hey, voting means actually going: the line 13 metro is packed, the polling station is at the back of a school, and the official at the table looks at you like you owe him money. Me, I’d have won without even leaving the house. Thank you for your attention, that is, if you’ve got five minutes, because I’m in a hurry.

In plain English

He says he would have won enormously (“wicked” means “very” in Boston) and compares it to 2004, when the Red Sox came back from three games down to beat the Yankees.

He waves away the polls, recalls that Boston was arguing about taxes before the United States existed (the Boston Tea Party, 1773), and leaves in a hurry: he’s double-parked.

Mind you, we know a thing or two about results up here. We gave the world the television you watched them on, and the telephone you’d have phoned to complain. Anyone who says I wouldn’t have won? Aye, right. Thanks for your attention to this matter.

In plain English

He wasn’t a candidate, but says he would have won a “proper” landslide, not a “wee” (small) one.

He reminds everyone that Scots gave the world the television (John Logie Baird) and the telephone (Alexander Graham Bell, born in Edinburgh), and answers any doubters with “aye, right”: the famous Scottish double positive that means “no chance”.
