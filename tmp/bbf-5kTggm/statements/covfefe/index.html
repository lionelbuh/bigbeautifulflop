The presidential version

The presidential version · satire

Despite persistent criticism in the press, my administration remains focused on the work the American people elected us to do. A free press is entitled to its criticism, and we are entitled to answer it with results.

Correction: an earlier message from this account was sent incomplete. We regret the error.

What changed

  • Finishes the sentence.
  • Treats the press as a fact of democracy rather than an enemy.
  • Corrects the mistake instead of letting the spokesman explain it.

The Swiss edition

Switzerland: edition of the day

Fribourgeois Fribourg, La Gruyère

Fribourgeois, in the original

Salut les gaillards ! Malgré tout ce que les journaux racontent sur moi à longueur de journée… covfefe.

Bon. Ceux d’en dehors du canton ont cru à une faute de frappe. Mais chez nous, en Gruyère, après une Bénichon, trois verres de vin et la moutarde de Bénichon, tout le monde parle comme ça, et personne ne demande de traduction.

D’ailleurs, de l’autre côté de la Sarine, ils ont compris tout de suite. Ils ont dit « Genau ». Allez, on finit la moitié-moitié avant qu’elle refroidisse.

The English adaptation

Hey folks! Despite everything the papers say about me all day long… covfefe.

Now, people from outside the canton thought it was a typo. But here in Gruyère, after a harvest festival, three glasses of wine and the festival’s sweet mustard, everybody talks like that, and nobody asks for a translation.

Besides, across the river, in the German-speaking part, they got it right away. They said “Exactly.” Come on, let’s finish the fondue before it gets cold.

Lost in translation: the notes
  • The Bénichon is Fribourg’s autumn harvest festival: a meal of many courses served with a sweet, spiced “moutarde de Bénichon”.
  • The Sarine river roughly marks the language border: French on one bank, German on the other. “Genau” means “exactly”.
  • Moitié-moitié (“half and half”) is the Fribourg fondue: half Gruyère, half Vacherin fribourgeois.

The French edition

France: edition of the day

Ch’ti Nord: Lille, Valenciennes

Ch’ti, in the original

Eh biloute ! Malgré tous chés journalisses qui dis’tent du mal ed mi tout l’temps… covfefe.

Ch’est pas un mot, ch’est un éternuement. Avec l’drache qu’on a eue toute la semaine, t’aurais été enrhumé aussi.

Allez, viens boire un jus à la maison, on va pas en faire un fromage. Même pas un maroilles.

The English adaptation

Hey, buddy! Despite all those journalists badmouthing me all the time… covfefe.

It’s not a word, it’s a sneeze. With the downpour we’ve had all week, you’d have caught a cold too.

Come on, come over for a coffee, let’s not make a big cheese out of it. Not even a stinky one.

Lost in translation: the notes
  • “Biloute” is an affectionate Ch’ti word for a friend, a kid, or anyone you like.
  • “La drache” is a heavy downpour, a regular feature of northern French weather.
  • “En faire un fromage” means to make a fuss. Maroilles is the Nord’s famously pungent cheese.
  • In the North, “un jus” is a coffee, and an invitation home comes faster than the rain.
